Why this data

Wildfire is now Canada's most visible environmental problem, and 2023 was the largest season on record. The headlines rarely show the longer pattern. We used the Canadian National Fire Database (CNFDB), maintained by the Canadian Forest Service, Natural Resources Canada: every reported fire since 1950 with its location, date, final size and cause, plus mapped burn perimeters from 1972 to 2024.

Each chart below makes one point and answers one of four questions:

  1. Is the area burned each year increasing, and how unusual was 2023?
  2. How do lightning- and human-caused fires differ in when, where and how big they burn?
  3. Do a few very large fires account for most of the area, and has the typical fire grown?
  4. Where are Canada's wildfire hotspots, and have they shifted?
Q1

Trend and the 2023 season

Fewer fires, more land burned

The number of fires reported each year peaked in the early 1990s and has fallen since. The area they burn has not followed: the 10-year average has kept climbing, and 2023 burned 17.6 million hectares, about twice the next-worst year.

Q2

Lightning vs people

Two causes, two different problems

People start most fires, but they start them where they can be reached and put out. Lightning starts fewer fires, in remote forest in midsummer, and those are the fires that grow.

Q3

Extremes

A few huge fires do almost all the burning

Most fires are put out small: 72% never reach one hectare. The area burned comes from the rare fires that escape, and the escaped lightning fires have been getting larger.

Q4

Geography

One boreal fire belt

Fifty years of burn scars trace a single belt of boreal forest from Yukon to Quebec. Scrub through the decades to see the scars accumulate. The most recent ones concentrate in the Northwest Territories, northern Alberta and Saskatchewan, and northern Quebec.

Conclusions

What the data says

Q1

Burning more with fewer fires

The 10-year average area burned has risen since the 1990s while fire counts fell. 2023 was an outlier in size (17.6 Mha) and in timing (a record May). Evidence: Figures 1–3.

Q2

Different causes, different fires

Human fires are frequent, spring-time and close to people. Lightning fires are fewer, midsummer and remote, and cause 84% of the area burned. Evidence: Figures 4–6 and 10.

Q3

The tail is the story

Fires of 200 ha or more are 4.8% of fires but 98.9% of the area. The median escaped lightning fire grew from 6 ha to 50 ha between the 1990s and 2020s. Evidence: Figures 7–8.

Q4

A stable belt, a shifting focus

The boreal belt has stayed in place, but recent decades burned hardest in the NWT, the northern Prairies and northern Quebec. Evidence: Figures 9–10.

Method and limits

How we built it

Data

  • CNFDB point data (448,447 fires, 1950–2025) and burned-area polygons (1972–2024), Natural Resources Canada, Open Government Licence – Canada.
  • Province boundaries: Natural Earth (public domain).

Cleaning decisions

  • Dropped year codes of −999 and 171 sparse pre-1950 records.
  • Unknown causes are kept and shown in grey.
  • Several agencies report fully only from the 1970s–80s, so earlier years are shaded.
  • Fire sizes are often recorded at 0.1 ha precision, so size trends use fires of 1 ha or more.

Tools

  • Python: pandas, Matplotlib and Seaborn for the printed figures; Plotly Express for this site; GeoPandas for the maps.
  • Every figure is reproducible from one notebook and one figure module.
